# Disputes

## What happens when a customer raises a dispute?

The assignment pauses and staff review it: the mission, the quote the customer accepted, the
messages, the evidence and the report. You are notified and can provide your account.

Payout is held while it is open. The review is about what was agreed against what was
delivered, not about who is more persuasive.

## How should I respond?

Factually, and with references. Point to the scope line, the exclusion, the message where
something was agreed, the evidence item that supports the finding under challenge.

Resist the urge to argue at length. A short response that cites four specific things in the
record is far stronger than a long one that relitigates the relationship.

## What do staff actually assess?

Whether what you delivered matches what the accepted quote said you would deliver. That is
the central question, and it is why the quote does so much work.

They also consider whether you communicated — a problem raised early and documented is
treated very differently from the same problem discovered by the customer at delivery.

## What outcomes are possible?

A required revision, a partial refund, a full refund, or the dispute resolved in your
favour with the assignment completing normally.

Decisions are recorded with their reasoning, and both parties are told what it was.

## The customer is disputing a conclusion they did not want

Say so plainly, and show the evidence. You are not obliged to have found what a customer
hoped you would find.

An investigation that honestly reports it could not establish something has done its job.
State it in the limitations section at the time, not for the first time in the dispute — a
limitation recorded up front is a finding, whereas one produced afterward looks like an
excuse.

## The customer says the scope included something I do not think it did

This is the most common dispute, and it is decided by what the quote says.

If the quote is ambiguous, expect the ambiguity to be read against you — you wrote it. That
is the practical reason to write exclusions even when they feel unnecessary. If the quote is
clear, point to it and the matter usually ends there.

## How do I protect myself before a dispute happens?

Write scope, assumptions and exclusions that a stranger could adjudicate. Keep every
conversation on-platform. Update the customer when something material changes. Record
limitations as you encounter them rather than at the end.

Essentially everything that protects you in a dispute is done before there is one.

## Does a dispute damage my standing?

A dispute raised is not a mark against you; disputes happen in this work. A pattern of
disputes resolved against you is a different signal, and it is one the platform acts on.

One dispute that went against you is not a crisis. Three in a row usually points at how you
are writing scope.

## Can I dispute something myself?

If a customer is unresponsive, refuses to complete an assignment you have delivered, or
behaves abusively, contact support with the assignment reference.

The formal dispute route is for customers, but staff can intervene either way, including
completing an assignment a customer has abandoned.
