# Dispute handling

## What am I deciding?

Whether what was delivered matches what the accepted quote said would be delivered. That is
the central question, and most disputes resolve on it alone.

You are not deciding who is more sympathetic, or whether the investigation found what the
customer hoped. You are comparing an agreement to a delivery.

## What do I look at, and in what order?

The accepted quote first — scope, deliverables, assumptions, exclusions, cancellation terms.
Then the report and evidence against it. Then the messages, for what was raised and when.

Reading the messages first is a common mistake. They are persuasive and they are not the
agreement.

## How do I treat an ambiguous quote?

Read it against the party who wrote it, which is the investigator. They chose the wording and
priced against it.

Note this explicitly in your reasoning. It is a consistent principle, and recording it means
the next person handling a similar dispute reaches the same result.

## The customer disputes a conclusion rather than a delivery

Not a ground on its own. An investigation that honestly reports it could not establish
something has performed the work.

Check whether the limitation was recorded at the time or only raised in the dispute. A
limitation stated in the report is a finding; one produced afterward is worth less.

## What outcomes can I give?

A required revision, a partial refund, a full refund, or resolution in the investigator's
favour with the assignment completing normally.

Prefer the narrowest outcome that resolves the actual problem. A revision that delivers the
missing element serves both parties better than a refund that leaves the customer with
nothing.

## How do I calculate a partial refund?

Against the work actually performed and the cancellation terms in the accepted quote. Show
your working in the decision.

Do not split the difference to close the case. A recorded rationale is what makes the
decision reviewable and what makes similar disputes consistent.

## What do I record?

The decision, the reasoning, and what you relied on. Both parties are told the outcome and
the reason.

Write it for someone reading it a year later with no memory of the case — including,
potentially, in a chargeback contest or legal proceeding.

## Do I need to access the evidence?

Only if the dispute turns on it. If the question is whether a deliverable exists, the report
and the assignment record usually answer it.

Evidence access requires a grant, is recorded, and is visible to the customer. Open it when
the decision needs it, not as a matter of routine.

## What if a party becomes abusive during the dispute?

Handle the abuse separately from the merits. Abusive conduct does not decide the dispute in
the other party's favour.

Report it through the abuse process and continue the review on its facts.

## What if the dispute suggests a pattern rather than an incident?

Resolve the dispute in front of you, then escalate the pattern separately.

Pattern handling — thresholds, what triggers a wider review — is in the internal operations
handbook, not here.
